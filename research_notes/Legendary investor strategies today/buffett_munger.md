## Core principles and how they evolved

### Takeaway
Buffett started as a Graham-style "cigar-butt" bargain hunter. Under Munger's influence he moved to buying "wonderful companies at fair prices" with durable moats, and holding them for decades. The fixed parts of the system are: a margin of safety, staying inside the circle of competence, no meaningful leverage, contempt for market timing and forecasting, opportunistic use of a large cash reserve when others panic, and (from Munger) mental models, inversion and the study of misjudgment. The post-Buffett team under Greg Abel says it keeps the same framework. In 2026 it has deployed more cash than Buffett did in his last years, mainly through buybacks, a homebuilder acquisition, Alphabet and Japan.

### Cited Findings
**Classic principles (background, primary doc not re-fetched):**
- "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." Buffett credits Munger with moving him off Graham's cigar-butt approach. — [Berkshire 1989 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html); see also the Munger tribute section in [2014 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf)

- Derivatives are "financial weapons of mass destruction". — [2002 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2002pdf.pdf)
- Gold is an unproductive asset: "it will remain lifeless forever". Productive businesses and farmland are preferred. — [2011 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2011ltr.pdf)
- Advice for ordinary investors and his estate's trustee: 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund and 10% in short-term government bonds. — [2013 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2013ltr.pdf)
- "Be fearful when others are greedy..." He bought U.S. stocks during the 2008 panic ("Buy American. I Am."). — [NYT op-ed, Oct 17, 2008](https://www.nytimes.com/2008/10/17/opinion/17buffett.html)
- Market cap to GNP is "probably the best single measure of where valuations stand at any given moment" (the "Buffett indicator"). — [Fortune, Dec 2001](https://money.cnn.com/magazines/fortune/fortune_archive/2001/12/10/314691/index.htm)
- Munger's "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (1995 talk; in Poor Charlie's Almanack) sets out about 25 biases, inversion ("invert, always invert") and a "latticework of mental models". — background, primary doc not re-fetched

### Inferences
- The evolution runs from cheap-and-mediocre (Graham) to quality-at-fair-price (Munger, See's), and then to a few huge concentrated bets (Coke, AmEx, Apple). Japan in 2020–2026 is a hybrid: cheap, high-yield, quality conglomerates funded with yen debt.
- With the indicator at about 250–260% and Buffett publicly calling the market a casino, the classic Buffett playbook points to patience, a big T-bill reserve and selective buying. It does not point to shorting or calling a top. Buffett has always said he does not time markets.

## Famous trades and what they show

### Takeaway
Each signature trade shows one principle. See's: pricing power. Coca-Cola and AmEx: moat plus buying during temporary trouble. GEICO: low-cost moat plus insurance float. 2008 Goldman/GE preferreds: being the liquidity provider in a panic, on terms rich in safety. Apple: a consumer-franchise view of "tech". BYD: Munger's long-range bet, sold after the run-up. Japan: global value plus yen-funded carry. Occidental and OxyChem: energy and real assets. Airlines and banks: willingness to exit when the moat thesis breaks.

### Inferences
- The trades show capital going where someone needs Berkshire's liquidity or patience (2008 preferreds, Occidental, OxyChem, Taylor Morrison). Berkshire sells when a valuation runs far ahead (BYD, Apple trims) or the thesis weakens (KHC, airlines 2020, BofA).
- Re-entering airlines in 2026 (Delta, see below) partly reverses Buffett's 2020 exit. It is a strong signal that the Abel/Combs/Weschler-era portfolio will take positions Buffett had sworn off.

## Known views on gold, crypto, airlines, tech/AI, index funds, bonds/T-bills

### Takeaway
Long-standing positions: no gold, no crypto (Munger: "rat poison" and "venereal disease"), index funds for ordinary investors, and short-term T-bills as the reserve, never long bonds. Tech has moved from "outside our circle" to the largest exposure: Apple, and now Alphabet, which is the third-largest position by mid-2026. On AI, Berkshire's stated stance is cautious and practical ("won't pursue AI for its own sake"), yet about 37% of the portfolio is reportedly concentrated in three tech giants.

## Recent Berkshire actions 2024–2026 (cash, buybacks, 13F trades, leadership)

### Takeaway
Berkshire was a net seller of stocks for about 13 straight quarters (2023–2025). Cash and T-bills hit a record $381.7B in Q3 2025, and there were no buybacks from May 2024 to Feb 2026. In 2026, under CEO Greg Abel (effective Jan 1, 2026), it began deploying: buybacks of about $0.23B in Q1, $4.5B in Q2 and a record $9.6B in Q3 (about $14.4B YTD). It closed OxyChem ($9.5B) and Taylor Morrison ($6.8B equity / $8.5B EV), cut the stock portfolio to about 29 names, built Alphabet into a top-3 holding and re-entered airlines. Cash is still huge: $377.4B headline at Sept 30, 2026, or about $352–357B adjusted.
